When I launched NaijaPrep, a developer friend asked why I was "limiting" myself to Nigerian students.

I didn't have a clean answer then. I do now.

## The problem with "build for everyone"

Products built for everyone are usually built for no one in particular.

The features are generic. The copy is vague. The assumptions are wrong for most of the real users. You end up with a product that works on paper but doesn't feel like it was made for you.

Many of the best products started hyper-local. WhatsApp was built for people who couldn't afford SMS. M-Pesa was built for Kenyans without bank accounts. Flutterwave exists because African payment infrastructure was broken. All of them scaled globally because they first solved a real problem for a specific group of people.

## What Nigeria taught me about engineering

Building for Nigeria forces you to solve hard problems.

- **Unreliable internet.** NaijaPrep works fully offline because many students don't have a stable connection. That constraint made me learn service workers, IndexedDB and sync strategies that most developers never touch. I can now build offline-first apps better than most.
- **Low-end devices.** When your users are on entry-level Android phones, you can't be lazy about performance. Bundle size matters. Image optimisation matters. Lazy loading is not optional. These habits make everything I build faster for everyone.
- **Cost sensitivity.** When users pay with limited data and limited income, every needless API call and every bloated dependency costs them something real. It made me a more disciplined engineer.

The constraints that look like limits are actually training.

## The products

NaijaPrep came from watching students struggle with scattered resources and no structured digital tools for JAMB and WAEC. I knew the problem personally, I knew what was missing, and I built it.

StudentHub NG came from the same place. Students needed more than questions. They needed analytics, streaks, community and a platform that felt modern and respected their intelligence.

StoreJet came from watching small vendors in Nigeria with no easy way to sell online. No technical skills, no budget for a developer, no time to learn an e-commerce platform. Sixty seconds to a store, WhatsApp native, built for how Nigerian vendors actually work.

NairaNest came from wanting to show what modern Nigerian digital banking could look like. Not a clone of something foreign, but something designed for how Nigerians think about money.

Every one of these came from observation, not inspiration.

## The global part

When you solve a real problem really well for a specific market, the skills transfer. The architecture patterns transfer. The discipline transfers.

A developer who can build an offline-first PWA that performs on a 2G connection can build almost anything. A developer who has shipped a real product to real users in a cost-sensitive market understands things that tutorials don't teach.

And the problems are not as unique as they look. Unreliable internet is not only a Nigerian problem. Cost sensitivity is not only a Nigerian problem. The need for accessible financial tools is not only a Nigerian problem.

Build something real for someone specific. Learn what it takes to actually solve their problem. Then see how transferable those skills become.

## What I tell other Nigerian developers

Stop waiting for permission to build something relevant to where you are.

You understand this market deeply: the users, the constraints, the culture, the infrastructure. That is an advantage, not a limitation. You have context that no developer in Silicon Valley can copy from the outside.

Use it.

Build the product you wish existed. Solve the problem you can actually see. Ship it before it is perfect. Learn from real users.

That is how every great product started. Geography doesn't decide it. Observation and execution do.
