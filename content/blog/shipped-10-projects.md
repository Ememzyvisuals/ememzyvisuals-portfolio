There is a developer somewhere right now who has been "almost ready to launch" for six months.

The codebase is 90% done. The design looks good. But something keeps stopping them. One more feature. One more refactor. One more review. The launch date keeps moving and the product never ships.

I know this developer because I used to be this developer.

Then I stopped, and everything changed.

## The lie we tell ourselves

"I'll launch when it's ready."

Ready is a moving target. The moment you get close to it, it moves. There is always one more edge case, one more screen to polish, one more API call to optimise.

Waiting to be ready is the most productive way to never ship anything.

I learned this by watching projects rot on my own machine. Perfect code that nobody used. Fully designed screens that never reached a real phone. Clever solutions to problems that real users never got to have solved.

## What shipping taught me

The first real project I shipped was terrifying. It had bugs. The UI was not perfect. Features I planned were missing. I put it out anyway.

Within 48 hours I learned more about what the product needed than in two months of building alone. Real users found problems I never imagined. They used features in ways that made no sense to me until I understood their context. They ignored things I thought were important and loved things I almost cut.

That feedback was worth more than any amount of solo polishing.

Every project since then has followed the same pattern. Ship the core. Learn from real use. Iterate fast. The products that look polished now look that way because of dozens of improvements after launch, not before it.

## The projects

NaijaPrep went live before I had every subject covered. Students told me which subjects they needed most, and I built those first.

AethLife launched before the AI correlation engine was fully optimised. Real usage showed me which correlations people actually wanted to see.

ClaudGPT shipped before all 9 agents were calibrated. Early users told me which agent failures annoyed them most, and I fixed those first.

In every case, shipping early made the product better than waiting would have.

## The maths is simple

A shipped product that is 80% good gets real feedback, real users and real improvement.

An unshipped product that is 95% good gets none of that.

The 80% product will end up better than the 95% product, because it had the one ingredient the other lacked: contact with reality.

## What self-taught developers know

Self-taught developers don't have a curriculum that tells us when we are ready. No professor signs off. No certificate says "you may now build real things."

So we either ship or we wait forever.

The ones who ship figure it out. The ones who wait for permission that never comes stay stuck.

Being self-taught means you already decided you don't need someone else to tell you when you are ready. Apply the same logic to your products.

## Ship it

Whatever you are building right now, if the core works, it is ready enough.

Put it in front of real people. Watch what happens. Fix what breaks. Add what is missing. Remove what nobody uses.

That is the whole process. Everything else is procrastination with better lighting.
